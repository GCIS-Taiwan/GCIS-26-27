Educational Philosophy
TL;DR Summary
  • Pedagogical Approach: Social constructivist and inquiry-based educator with a foundation in Early Childhood Education and Psychology.
  • Classroom Role: Facilitator who reduces direct instruction to foster peer learning, student agency, and problem-solving.
  • Community & Well-being: Prioritises emotional safety and strong relationships.

Constructivist Foundations & Facilitation

My philosophy aligns with Piaget’s belief that every time we teach children something, we keep them from inventing it themselves. I also think that students learn best from their peers. I see the role of the teacher as reducing reliance on direct instruction and acting more as a facilitator in the learning process and manager of the information students need to learn. There will be times students do need direct instruction, but I will only take that path when all other avenues have been tried.

Inquiry-Based Learning & Scaffolding

I am a passionate believer in inquiry. As Kath Murdoch, one of the foremost advocates of the technique, states, it takes a special kind of teacher to become an inquiry-based teacher—someone who is willing to give agency to the students to let them power the learning process. As a teacher, you have to know when is the right time to be in charge, and when is the right time to let go. It also takes a special skill to know what type of scaffolding, or support, is needed at the right time to guide students through the learning process. Often, it comes down to asking the right questions.

Psychology, Safety, and Classroom Culture

Having completed a degree in psychology before pursuing a teaching career, I bring that understanding into my interactions with people. Because I believe that students learn best from their peers, learning occurs best when students feel safe and secure. As a teacher, I take the time to run the Circle Solutions Program. The benefits of this approach mean that all members of the learning community get to know each other at a deeper level, leading to a more compassionate and caring learning environment.

International Mindedness & Student Action

As an educator, several learning practices are fundamental to learning in an increasingly connected world. Being internationally minded is of great importance. Learning must be significant and meaningful for learners if they are expected to be engaged. When we show students the connections their learning has to the world, and their potential to solve problems of a global nature, they become more empowered and motivated.

A focus on key concepts is another way we can help learners see the "bigger picture." Enabling students to take action is one of the most rewarding aspects of learning once established properly. When you create a framework where units are significant and meaningful, students take action of their own accord. You can read an example of this approach in practice on my blog post regarding student-led inquiry and visible thinking.

Assessment for Growth

One of the most rewarding aspects of teaching in a learning environment that values the needs of learners is following best practices as they apply to assessment. I follow a process where I first discover what students already know about an area of inquiry, then identify which aspects the learners find most interesting.

Together, we create learning engagements and activities that allow students to display their understanding. These engagements align with their developmental stages and individual needs while remaining open-ended enough so that students can achieve at their own level. Guiding this process is a primary goal, ensuring learners understand the purpose of learning by making goals explicit and providing clear examples. Formative assessments ensure continuous awareness of each learner's progress, enhanced by constructive and timely feedback. Learners are empowered to control their own learning—setting goals, monitoring progress, and reflecting on future steps to improve.

Lifelong Learning

I am a lifelong learner. The world is changing at an astronomical rate, and education must evolve just to remain relevant. I believe that a focus on skills is essential, as there is no way we can predict all the specific information students will need when they leave formal education.
